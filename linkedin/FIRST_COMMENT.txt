Three questions I expect, answered up front.

"SQuAD is public, so the model has already seen it."

Probably, and that is why the third slice exists. The easy negatives are questions paired with a passage from a different article, so those combinations have never existed anywhere and cannot have been memorised. Here is the part that matters though: if memorisation were doing the work, the hard negatives, which ARE the public dataset, should have been the easy ones. They were the hardest by 30 points. The difficulty is real.

"600 items is not many."

Correct. It is enough to establish the gap between slices, which is large and consistent. It is thin for the per-bucket calibration detail, where some middle buckets hold only 13 to 20 items. I would not quote those individual buckets as anything but directional.

"Why no comparison against a normal LLM?"

No API access to a frontier model. Rather than quote someone else's benchmark as if it were mine, the comparison here is Jev's confident slice against its uncertain slice. That tells you how much traffic it can own; it tells you nothing about whether GPT or Claude would do better on the same items. That is the biggest gap in the work and it is stated in the repo.

Method, briefly: one question per request, sequential, identical instruction text fixed before the run. Batching into one call is cheaper and is what production code should do, but it would make per decision latency meaningless. The calibration noise floor is resampled rather than assumed, because a raw 0.047 reads like miscalibration until you know a perfect model scores 0.039 on the same sample size.

Everything is reproducible, including the raw per item responses, so you can recompute any figure without spending anything:
https://github.com/ManankumarThakkar/jev-escalation-gate
