The finding I didn't expect 👇

6 of the 10 high-confidence false acceptances had potentially incorrect benchmark labels.

What struck me more: confidence and label quality moved together. Above 0.90, most of the apparent errors looked like the benchmark's. Between 0.50 and 0.90, they looked real, and they were overwhelmingly one thing: a question subtly altered against the passage.

So the model's confidence was carrying information even where the labels said it was wrong. That is not something I would have found from an accuracy number.

A reminder that evaluating a model also means evaluating the data you're measuring it against.
